Interactive problems.
Computed fields. Checkable answers.

Each experiment sets a quantitative target under constraints. Change the geometry and the conditions, run a real solver, explore the field it computes — and find out whether you met the target, and how far the numbers can be trusted.

How it works

Two ways to compute the same thing

The same problem can be solved quickly and approximately, or slowly and more faithfully. The lab offers both routes and always tells you which one you are looking at — because the difference between them is part of what there is to learn.

  • Fast preview — a regular Cartesian grid solved in NumPy. Near-instant, and approximate by construction: the grid does not follow the body's outline.
  • FEniCSx computation — an unstructured triangular mesh generated with Gmsh, solved with P1 finite elements. Slower, more faithful close to the body, and it produces a VTK file that opens in ParaView.
  • Panel method — for the airfoil, neither of the above: source and vortex sheets on the body itself, so the boundary is exact rather than meshed. It is not a preview of something better; it is the most accurate surface solution here.

How it is made

The lab is an application built on Fenix Spoon, an open-source toolkit that puts a finite-element solver behind a web page: the protocol, the job handling, the solvers and the browser widgets all come from there. What you find here is the teaching experience built on top — the problems, the explanations, the design and the public deployment.

The code for this lab is on GitHub under the MIT licence. The real computations are carried out by FEniCSx, and the fast previews and the panel method by NumPy.
